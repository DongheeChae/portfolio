About

Senior at Korea University — health and environmental science, with a second degree in biomedical engineering. I spent 2025–2026 at the University of Iowa in the Ankrum Lab, where I ran my first project end to end. Now in the Nano-Biomedicine Lab at Korea University.

Two ideas hold the work together. Regulated cell death is not an endpoint but an instruction the immune system reads. And an instruction is only useful if it arrives in one place, at one time — so I build particles as well as assays.

The environmental training is not a detour. Exposure science taught me to treat immunity as something acted on from outside, which is exactly what I now ask of lead and PCBs in antigen-presenting cells.

Channel 01

Stem cell-based immunomodulation

MSCs are already used as an immunosuppressive therapy, but much of what they do happens after they die. I work on the handoff: what a monocyte becomes once it has eaten a dying MSC, and how far that state travels.

    Questions this channel asks
  • Does licensing an MSC before it dies change the immune state of the cell that clears it?
  • Does that state survive the transition from monocyte to macrophage?
  • Can it spread to macrophages that never touched an MSC?

Cytokine pre-licensing of MSCs and monocyte efferocytosis

Ankrum Lab, University of Iowa · PI: James Ankrum · Aug 2025 – May 2026
First author · under review

Pre-licensing is the standard way to strengthen an MSC product before infusion — but it is always evaluated on the living cell. I asked what it does to the cells that clear the MSC afterwards.

Pre-licensed MSCs drove a larger efferocytic response from primary human monocytes, and the macrophages those monocytes became were more immunosuppressive, measurable as reduced T-cell proliferation.

What I did

Wrote the protocols, ran the whole pipeline from primary cell isolation through multicolour flow cytometry, and led manuscript preparation. The five-colour panel (CD14, CD16, CD86, CD163, CD206) is my own design, including spillover planning and all FlowJo analysis.

Channel 02

Regulated cell death and engineered nanomaterials

A drug that works everywhere is toxic everywhere — IL-2 is the textbook case. This channel builds the particle that decides where and when the instruction arrives, and what kind of death it triggers.

    Questions this channel asks
  • Can radiation itself be the trigger that switches a drug on, only inside the field?
  • Is it the amount of iron a cell takes in, or the amount per carrier, that decides ferroptosis?
  • Can a carrier reprogram the macrophages already inside a tumour instead of engineering T cells outside the body?

Radiation-activated immunotherapy: scintillating nanoparticles

Nano-Biomedicine Lab, Korea University · with KIRAMS and Harvard Medical School
Lead on synthesis

Systemic IL-2 reached the clinic and was stopped by toxicity. Keep the biology, remove the geography problem: load an inactivated IL-2-mimetic onto a particle, and let the radiation beam already aimed at the tumour switch it on.

The particle is a core/shell lanthanide scintillator (NaLuF₄:Nd/Tm@NaYF₄, LaF₃:Ce@LaF₃) that turns X-ray energy into UV. That UV cleaves the linker holding the payload inactive — so the drug switches on only inside the irradiated field.

What I did

Lead the particle synthesis and authored the lab synthesis protocol. Also designed the in silico repurposing workflow used to find the IL-2-mimetic the particle will carry.

Core/shell synthesisLanthanide dopingUV-labile linkerDLS / ELSX-ray irradiation

Iron payload as the ferroptosis switch

Nano-Biomedicine Lab, Korea University
In progress

Ferroptosis is iron-dependent, and most delivery work optimizes how much particle a cell takes up. This tests a different variable: iron per internalized carrier. MSN–carboxydextran–iron oxide composites let uptake stay roughly constant while payload density varies, in MC38 colorectal carcinoma cells.

What I did

Surface chemistry, particle characterization, and the full assay panel: viability, intracellular ROS, Ferrozine iron quantification.

HER2-ADC response prediction from trafficking biology

Nano-Biomedicine Lab, Korea University × KIST molecular imaging
Analysis complete

HER2 level is the standard biomarker for trastuzumab deruxtecan — but an ADC only works if it is internalized, trafficked, and cleaved. Two tumours with identical HER2 can behave differently for reasons downstream of binding.

I built an eight-module, 59-gene framework spanning receptor binding through lysosomal degradation and drug efflux, then screened fifteen GEO datasets in R. Cathepsin and ESCRT-family genes emerged as the strongest discriminators of response.

What I did

Built the framework, curated the modules, wrote the R analysis, and prioritized the candidates now entering imaging validation.

RGEO / bulk RNA-seqDifferential expressionMolecular imaging

In silico drug repurposing for an IL-2-mimetic payload

Nano-Biomedicine Lab, Korea University
Screening under way

The IL-2 receptor is a hard target: three subunits, a flat interface, and recognition driven by scattered hotspot residues rather than one pocket. Designing a peptide mimic is difficult for exactly that reason.

So search what already exists. The workflow screens an approved-drug library against AlphaFold-derived structures, uses a known binder as positive control, filters on hotspot occupancy, then narrows survivors with precision docking before anything reaches a cell.

What I did

Designed the workflow with the computational team, arguing for two stages — broad virtual screening first, Rosetta-grade refinement only on the shortlist — so candidates reaching the bench are already defensible.

Lead (Pb) and MHC class I cross-presentation in dendritic cells

Nano-Biomedicine Lab, Korea University · BRL hazardous substances project
Study designed

Low-dose lead pushes aberrant MHC class II to the macrophage surface by increasing vesicular exocytosis and autophagosome formation. What nobody has shown is whether that disturbance leaves antigen processing intact.

The study asks whether non-cytotoxic Pb impairs phagolysosomal processing, MHC class I cross-presentation, and antigen-specific CD8⁺ T-cell activation in DC2.4 dendritic cells.

What I did

Designed the study and wrote the proposal: rationale, dose selection below the cytotoxic threshold, and the readout panel.
